# Acute Leukaemia: Comprehensive Overview

## Definition

Acute leukaemia is a group of aggressive haematologic malignancies characterised by the rapid, uncontrolled proliferation of immature haematopoietic cells (blast cells) in the bone marrow and peripheral blood. Unlike chronic leukaemias, which progress slowly and involve more mature cell populations, acute leukaemias are defined by the accumulation of poorly differentiated blast cells that fail to mature into functional blood cells. This leads to progressive bone marrow failure with resulting cytopenias (anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia).

Historically, acute leukaemia has been defined morphologically by the presence of **≥20% blast cells** in the bone marrow or peripheral blood. However, the **WHO 2022 (5th edition)** classification has fundamentally shifted toward a genetics-driven approach, eliminating the 20% blast requirement for AML with defining genetic abnormalities in most cases.

If untreated, acute leukaemia is invariably fatal, typically within weeks to months of diagnosis.

## Classification

### Two Major Types

#### 1. Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML)

A rapidly progressing myeloid neoplasm characterised by clonal expansion of primitive haematopoietic stem cells (myeloblasts) in the bone marrow. This results in ineffective erythropoiesis and megakaryopoiesis, manifesting as bone marrow failure with inadequate production of red blood cells, platelets, and functional white blood cells.

- **Epidemiology:** ~22,000 new cases annually in the United States (2025 estimates). Median age of onset is approximately 70 years, with a male predominance in older patients.
- **AML with defining genetic abnormalities** (WHO 2022): Includes subtypes defined by specific translocations and mutations such as:
  - AML with PML::RARA (acute promyelocytic leukaemia)
  - AML with RUNX1::RUNX1T1
  - AML with CBFB::MYH11
  - AML with NPM1 mutation
  - AML with CEBPA mutation
  - AML with MECOM rearrangement (new in WHO 2022)
  - AML with NUP98 rearrangement (new in WHO 2022)
- **AML defined by differentiation**: Classified by morphology and immunophenotype when no defining genetic abnormality is present.

#### 2. Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia (ALL)

A malignant clonal disease arising from lymphoid progenitor cells in the bone marrow and extramedullary sites. Early lymphoid precursors proliferate and replace normal haematopoietic cells.

- **Epidemiology:** The most common type of cancer and leukaemia in children.
- **B-cell ALL subtypes** (ICC 2022): Includes BCR::ABL1-positive B-ALL (subdivided into lymphoid-only and multilineage involvement), Philadelphia-like ALL, and others.
- **T-cell ALL subtypes**: Including early T-cell precursor (ETP) ALL, a high-risk subgroup.

#### Other Rare Subtypes

- Blastic Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell Neoplasm (BPDCN)
- Acute leukaemias of ambiguous lineage (mixed phenotype)
- Mature B-cell and T-cell leukaemias
- NK cell-related leukaemias

## WHO 2022 Classification: Paradigm Shift

The WHO 2022 classification represents a fundamental shift in how acute leukaemias are diagnosed:

### Key Changes
1. **Genetics over morphology**: AML with defining genetic abnormalities supersedes blast-count-based diagnosis. The 20% blast threshold has been largely eliminated for genetically defined subtypes.
2. **MDS/AML overlap category**: A new category recognising patients with 10–19% blasts in specific molecular contexts.
3. **Exceptions requiring ≥20% blasts**: AML with BCR::ABL1 (to distinguish from CML blast crisis) and AML with CEBPA mutation still require the traditional threshold.
4. **ICC 2022 comparison**: The International Consensus Classification takes a similar but slightly more conservative approach, requiring ≥10% blasts for most genetically defined subtypes.

### Clinical Trial Trends (2022–2025)
Use of the 10% blast threshold in clinical trials has increased from 4.0% (2022) to 25.8% (2025), while the 20% threshold has declined from 68.0% to 41.9%, reflecting the evolving classification landscape.

## Pathophysiology

Acute leukaemias arise from genetic mutations in haematopoietic stem or progenitor cells that confer:

1. **Proliferative advantage**: Mutations in signalling pathways (e.g., FLT3, RAS, KIT) drive uncontrolled cell division.
2. **Differentiation block**: Mutations affecting transcription factors (e.g., RUNX1, CEBPA) or epigenetic regulators prevent normal maturation.
3. **Impaired apoptosis**: Disruption of programmed cell death pathways (e.g., BCL-2 overexpression) allows survival of abnormal cells.
4. **Clonal dominance**: The leukaemic clone expands at the expense of normal haematopoiesis, leading to bone marrow failure.

### The "Two-Hit" Model
Leukaemogenesis often requires cooperation between at least two classes of mutations:
- **Class I mutations**: Confer proliferative advantage (e.g., FLT3-ITD, RAS mutations)
- **Class II mutations**: Impair differentiation (e.g., PML::RARA, RUNX1::RUNX1T1)

## Risk Factors

- **Ionising radiation exposure**
- **Chemical exposure**: Benzene, pesticides, polyaromatic hydrocarbons (tobacco smoke)
- **Prior chemotherapy**: Alkylating agents, topoisomerase II inhibitors (therapy-related AML)
- **Antecedent haematologic disorders**: Myelodysplastic syndromes (40–60% evolve to AML), myeloproliferative neoplasms including CMML
- **Genetic predisposition**: Down syndrome, Fanconi anaemia, Bloom syndrome, ataxia-telangiectasia, Li-Fraumeni syndrome, xeroderma pigmentosum

---

## Clinical Presentation

### Common Features
- **Anaemia**: Fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea
- **Thrombocytopenia**: Petechiae, bruising, bleeding
- **Neutropenia**: Recurrent or severe infections, fever
- **Bone pain**: Due to marrow expansion
- **Hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy** (more common in ALL)
- **Gingival hyperplasia** (characteristic of monocytic AML subtypes)
- **CNS involvement** (more common in ALL, monocytic AML)
- **Disseminated intravascular coagulation** (especially acute promyelocytic leukaemia)

---

## Diagnosis

1. **Complete blood count and peripheral blood smear**: Circulating blast cells, cytopenias
2. **Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy**: Morphologic assessment of blast percentage and dysplasia
3. **Immunophenotyping (flow cytometry)**: Determines lineage (myeloid vs. lymphoid) and maturation stage
4. **Cytogenetics (karyotyping, FISH)**: Identifies chromosomal translocations and abnormalities
5. **Molecular genetics (NGS, PCR)**: Detects gene mutations for classification, risk stratification, and targeted therapy selection
6. **Measurable residual disease (MRD)**: Increasingly important for monitoring treatment response and guiding post-remission therapy

---

## Treatment

### AML Treatment
- **Intensive induction chemotherapy**: Standard "7+3" regimen (cytarabine + daunorubicin) for fit patients; CR rates ~60–80% in younger adults
- **Hypomethylating agents (HMA) + venetoclax**: Standard for older/unfit patients; high CR rates with manageable toxicity
- **Targeted therapies**:
  - Midostaurin/quizartinib (FLT3-mutated AML)
  - Ivosidenib/enasidenib (IDH1/IDH2-mutated AML)
  - Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (CD33+ AML)
  - Revumenib (KMT2A-rearranged AML, approved 2024)
- **Allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT)**: For intermediate/high-risk patients in first remission
- **All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) + arsenic trioxide**: Curative for acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL)

### ALL Treatment
- **Multi-agent chemotherapy**: Induction, consolidation, and maintenance phases over 2–3 years
- **Tyrosine kinase inhibitors**: For Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL (combined with chemotherapy or blinatumomab)
- **Immunotherapy**:
  - Blinatumomab (bispecific T-cell engager)
  - Inotuzumab ozogamicin (antibody-drug conjugate)
  - CAR T-cell therapy (tisagenlecleucel for relapsed/refractory ALL)
- **Allo-SCT**: For high-risk patients or those with persistent MRD

## Prognosis & Survival

### AML
- **Overall 5-year survival**: ~29.5% (all ages)
- **Children**: ~85–90% achieve remission after induction; significantly better long-term outcomes
- **Younger adults (18–65)**: 3-year OS of 58–65% with modern intensive regimens
- **Older adults (>60)**: Significantly worse outcomes; treatment less effective

### ALL
- **Children (0–14 years)**: >95% achieve remission; 5-year survival ~90–93%
- **Adolescents (15–19 years)**: 5-year survival ~74%
- **Adults (≥25 years)**: 5-year survival ~25–50% depending on subtype and risk factors
- **Ph-positive ALL**: Now achieves 5-year survival >80% with TKI-based regimens

### Key Prognostic Factors
- **Age** (younger = better prognosis)
- **Cytogenetics and molecular profile** (the single most important factor)
- **MRD status** after induction therapy
- **Response to initial treatment** (achieving CR)
- **White blood cell count at diagnosis**
- **CNS involvement**

## Relevance to CMML

Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukaemia (CMML) has a well-documented propensity for transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia, termed **CMML blast phase (CMML-BP)**. This is one of the most clinically significant complications of CMML:

### CMML → AML Transformation
- **Frequency**: Approximately 15–30% of CMML patients transform to AML
- **Definition**: Blast count rises to ≥20% in blood or bone marrow
- **Molecular drivers**: RAS pathway mutations (NRAS, KRAS, CBL) are key drivers of proliferation and AML transformation. NPM1 and FLT3 mutations indicate potential for rapid progression.
- **Clonal evolution**: Transformation typically occurs through acquisition of additional mutations, particularly in signalling pathways, on top of the founding epigenetic and spliceosome mutations

### Outcomes of CMML-BP
- Outcomes are dismal compared to de novo AML
- AML arising from antecedent CMML is classified as secondary AML (sAML)
- **Treatment options**:
  - CPX-351 (liposomal daunorubicin/cytarabine) — specifically approved for secondary AML
  - Venetoclax-based combinations (HMA + venetoclax)
  - Allogeneic stem cell transplantation — offers modest survival advantage but bridging patients to transplant remains challenging
- **Emerging approaches**: Anti-CD123 therapies, MCL-1 inhibition, dual MEK/PLK1 inhibition, FLT3 inhibition for RAS/CBL-mutated subsets, LILRB4-targeting immunotherapy

### Clinical Vigilance
- An increasing blast count (>5% in blood or marrow) in CMML is a treatment indication and a warning sign of transformation
- Any CMML patient presenting with sudden clinical deterioration (new cytopenias, organ dysfunction, constitutional symptoms) should be evaluated for AML transformation
- The WHO 2022 diagnostic criterion of blasts <20% (including promonocytes) is what distinguishes CMML from acute monocytic leukaemia
